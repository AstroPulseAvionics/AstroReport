\section{Thrust Requirement}
One of the requirements was that "The system should be capable of producing a thrust level sufficient for orbit keeping of a nanosatellite at 200km altitude". To calculate the necessary thrust levels for maintaining a stable orbit at various altitudes, a python script was developed. The script considered atmospheric drag as the primary actor, while assuming all other forces such as solar radiation pressure were negligible. The drag force was calculated using the standard drag equation:

\begin{equation}
    F_d = \frac{1}{2} C_d \rho A v^2
\end{equation}

where \( F_d \) is the drag force, \( C_d \) is the drag coefficient, \( \rho \) is the atmospheric density at the given altitude, \( A \) is the cross-sectional area of the satellite, and \( v \) is the orbital velocity.

The drag coefficient \( C_d \) was assumed to be 2.2, which is typical for small satellites. (Provide a reference for this) 
The cross-sectional area \( A \) was taken as the average surface area of the satellite.
The atmospheric density \( \rho \) was estimated using the ... model, which provides values for densities of each element in the atmosphere at different altitudes.
The orbital velocity \( v \) was calculated using the formula for circular orbits:

\begin{equation}
    v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
\end{equation}

where \( G \) is the gravitational constant, \( M \) is the mass of the Earth, and \( r \) is the distance from the center of the Earth to the satellite.


As shown in fig \ref{fig:drag_force_vs_altitude}, the script iterated over a range of altitudes and satellite sizes to compute the required thrust to counteract drag. The results were plotted to visualize the relationship between altitude, satellite size, and required thrust.

\section{Propulsion Methods Considered}

\subsection{Electrospray Thrusters}

Electrospray thrusters operate by electrostatically extracting ions or charged droplets from an ionic liquid propellant under high electric fields. The liquid surface forms Taylor cones at sharp emitter tips, from which charged particles are accelerated to generate thrust \cite{levchenko-propulsion_summary}. Ionic liquids such as 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (EMI-BF$_4$) and bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (EMI-Im) are commonly employed due to their negligible vapor pressure and storability at ambient conditions, making them attractive for CubeSat propulsion \cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}.

Electrospray thrusters typically generate thrust in the μN to few-mN range, with specific impulses generally in the ≈500–1800~s range (ionic-regime devices tend toward the upper end; e.g., the TILE demonstration showed ≈1800~s while typical MEMS and laboratory electrospray designs report ≈850–1263~s). \cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary,tummala-propulsion_summary,lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}. Their power requirements are modest, spanning from sub-watt operation to several tens of watts, which makes them compatible with CubeSat-scale power systems. The absence of a plasma discharge eliminates the need for cathodes or magnetic confinement systems, reducing overall system complexity compared to other plasma-based thrusters \cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}.

Despite these advantages, electrospray thrusters face challenges related to long-term emitter stability, wetting behavior, and contamination. Nevertheless, there is a growing flight heritage. MIT’s SiEPS and NASA’s iEPS thrusters have been demonstrated in-orbit, showing that electrosprays are a practical solution for precision CubeSat maneuvers \cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}.

\subsection{Gridded Ion Thrusters}

Gridded ion thrusters (GITs) generate thrust by ionizing a noble gas propellant, most commonly xenon, and accelerating the ions electrostatically through a system of precision-aligned grids. An external cathode supplies electrons to neutralize the ion beam, preventing spacecraft charging \cite{feep-plasma-propulsion}. These thrusters are among the most efficient electric propulsion systems, with overall efficiencies reaching 60–70\% in optimized designs \cite{fundamentals-of-electric-propulsion}.

Performance depends strongly on scale: miniature ion engines for nanosatellites can achieve thrust levels of $0.1$–$1.5$~mN at power inputs of $10$–$50$~W, with specific impulses ranging from $1000$–$3000$~s \cite{tummala-propulsion_summary}. Larger spacecraft-class thrusters, such as those used in NASA’s Dawn mission, provide up to hundreds of mN of thrust at kilowatt power levels, with $I_{sp}$ exceeding 4000~s \cite{bpt-4000}. While xenon is the dominant propellant, alternatives such as krypton and iodine have also been tested for CubeSat and low-cost applications \cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}. 

System complexity is relatively high: GITs require ionization chambers, multi-grid assemblies, beam neutralizers, and precise power management. However, their flight heritage is unmatched — with a history dating back to SERT-1 in 1964 and extending through numerous deep space and Earth-orbiting missions. Recent CubeSat-scale demonstrations, such as the University of Tokyo’s $\mu$1 thruster on HODOYOSHI-4, validate their application in nanosatellite contexts \cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}.

\subsection{Vacuum Arc Thrusters}

Vacuum arc thrusters (VATs) are pulsed propulsion devices that generate thrust by initiating an arc discharge on a solid cathode, commonly titanium. The discharge vaporizes and ionizes cathode material at localized arc spots, producing a plasma plume expelled at high velocity \cite{schein-vat}. Since the electrode itself serves as both propellant and discharge source, VATs require no external propellant feed system, which simplifies integration for small spacecraft.

VATs typically provide impulse bits of tens of $\mu$N·s, with average thrust levels from tens of $\mu$N to sub-mN depending on pulse frequency and electrode material. Their specific impulse usually falls between 1000–3000~s, though performance depends strongly on cathode composition \cite{chowdhury-vat}. Power requirements are low on average but involve large instantaneous discharge currents, which places demands on pulsed power electronics. 

System complexity is relatively low compared to gas-fed thrusters, though erosion of cathode surfaces and feeding mechanisms for long-duration operation remain key challenges. A deployable VAT system for CubeSat end-of-life disposal was proposed and demonstrated by Kim et al. \cite{kim-vat}, highlighting VATs as a candidate for orbital debris mitigation. Flight heritage includes the $\mu$CAT thruster aboard BRICSat-P and BRICSat-2, demonstrating successful on-orbit operation \cite{schein-vat}. Although less mature than ion or Hall thrusters, VATs are increasingly viable for compact, low-cost CubeSat propulsion.


\subsection{Field Emission Electric Propulsion (FEEP) Thrusters}

A subset of electrostatic propulsion, \ac{FEEP}, uses strong electrostatic fields to extract and accelerate charged particles, typically metal ions, from a liquid metal propellant, such as indium, cesium, or gallium \cite{feep-plasma-propulsion}. The fundamental mechanism involves the generation of extremely high electric fields (on the order of $10^9~\mathrm{V/m}$) at the sharp tips of field emission cathodes. These fields ionize the liquid metal and accelerates the resulting positive ions out of the emitter at high velocities, generating thrust \cite{feep-general-tip-design}\cite{feep-ion-emitter-study}. 

Flight-proven \ac{FEEP} systems, such as ENPULSION NANO and NanoFEEP \cite{tajmar-feep} demonstrate thrust levels randing from a few tens of $\mu$N up to 1.4 mN, with specific impulses exceeding 5000 s \cite{marcuccio-feep}\cite{tajmar-feep}\cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}. A wide thrust range, combined with best-in-class specific impulse, enables \ac{FEEP} thrusters to perform precise high $\Delta v$ maneuvers \cite{levchenko-propulsion_summary}\cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}. Power requirements for nano-FEEP thrusters can be as low as 50 mW but can be as high as 100 W based on a max thrust of 1.4 mN \cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}. 

\ac{FEEP}’s inherently high specific impulse reduces the required propellant mass of the thruster, which in turn facilitates miniaturization of the propulsion system \cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}. However, this performance comes at the expense of very low thrust levels, limiting their suitability for rapid orbit transfers. \cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}\cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}. The strict manufacturing tolerances required for the cathodic emitter tips, together with specialized propellant handling procedures, make \ac{FEEP} one of the most complex electric propulsion systems to develop and operate \cite{alnaqbi-propulsion_summary}. Nevertheless, their high efficiency and fine controllability affirm \ac{FEEP} thrusters as attractive options for nanosatellites requiring precision maneuvering capabilities.


\subsection{Hall Effect Thrusters}

One of the most popular forms of electric propulsion, Hall effect thrusters (HETs) use a combination of magnetic and electric fields to accelerate ions and produce thrust. A neutral propellant gas, typically xenon or krypton, is injected into a cylindrical chamber near the anode and is ionized by electrons trapped in an azimuthal Hall current. The resulting plasma, consisting of the propellant ions, is then accelerated axially by the applied electric field, from the anode toward the cathode near the outlet of the chamber, generating thrust \cite{tummala-propulsion_summary}\cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}\cite{levchenko-propulsion_summary}. Unlike grided ion engines, \ac{HET}s require no acceleration grids, avoiding grid erosion and improving operational lifetime. In terms of flight heritage, Hall effect thrusters are among the most mature electric propulsion systems, having flown on numerous commercial satellites and on missions such as ESA’s SMART-1 lunar transfer mission \cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}\cite{levchenko-propulsion_summary}.

Dependent on the available input power, micro-HETs developed for CubeSats can produce thrust levels in the 1–50 mN range with specific impulses typically between 1000 and 3000 s \cite{tummala-propulsion_summary}. These performance metrics scale directly with input power, which for current CubeSat-class Hall thrusters lies in the 10-600W range \cite{tummala-propulsion_summary}\cite{levchenko-propulsion_summary}. When compared to gridded ion thrusters, high thrust density can enable \ac{HET} engines to maneuver faster at a moderate $I_{sp}$, however, due to having a lower specific impulse, the same \ac{HET} engine needs a higher propellant mass for the same $\Delta v$ \cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}. Despite limitations in specific impulse and lifetime, \ac{HET}s offer a unique balance of thrust, efficiency, and flight heritage that makes their development less constrained by system complexity compared to many other electric propulsion technologies.

\subsection{Micro-Pulsed Plasma Thrusters}

As the name suggests, pulsed plasma thrusters use short, high-current electrical discharges to ablate a solid propellant, commonly \ac{PTFE} or Teflon. The discharge current travelling through the ionized ablated propellant generates a magnetic field, and the interaction between this current and its self-induced magnetic field produces a Lorentz force. This force accelerates and expels the plasma out of the thruster, generating thrust \cite{lozano-smallspacecraft_summary}\cite{levchenko-propulsion_summary}. The current pulses typically last for a few microseconds and are driven by a capacitor that is charged approximately every one second \cite{tummala-propulsion_summary}.

Being pulsed devices, \ac{muPPT}s deliver thrust in short bursts, with average thrust levels in the $\mu$N range for single pulses and time-averaged thrusts of approximately 140 $\mu$N.
